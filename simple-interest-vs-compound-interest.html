Simple Interest vs Compound Interest: Formulas and Examples

Interest can grow your money — but depending on the method, the results can be very different. This guide compares the formulas with checked USD examples and shows how compounding frequency and time affect the outcome.

Quick answer: Simple interest earns interest only on the original amount (principal). Compound interest earns interest on the principal and past interest — so growth speeds up over time. If you want the full concept explained with more examples, start with our compound interest guide.

Simple Interest: The Basics

In this basic, non-amortizing example, simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. With a fixed principal and rate, the interest accumulates in a straight line.

Simple Interest Formula

I = P × r × t and A = P + I

P = original principal, r = annual rate as a decimal (5% = 0.05), t = time in years, I = simple interest, and A = final amount. Assumes no interim payments, withdrawals, fees, or taxes.

Example: $10,000 at 5% for 5 years (simple interest)

  • Interest each year: $10,000 × 0.05 = $500
  • Total interest after 5 years: $500 × 5 = $2,500
  • Final value: $10,000 + $2,500 = $12,500

Assuming the principal stays unchanged and no interest is added to it, the amount earned each year is constant.

When it’s useful: Simple interest is often used for quick estimates and some short-term borrowing scenarios. If you’re comparing quoted bank rates, it helps to understand the difference between APR and APY, because APY reflects the effect of compounding on deposit interest, while APR is a borrowing-cost measure with a different purpose.

Compound Interest: The Basics

With a positive fixed rate and no withdrawals, compound interest can grow your money exponentially.
You earn interest on the principal and on the interest you already earned.

Compound Interest Formula

A = P (1 + r/n)(n·t)

Where: P = principal, r = nominal annual rate as a decimal, n = compounding periods per year, t = years, and A = ending balance. Assumes a constant rate, no additional contributions, fees, or withdrawals.

Want to calculate this without doing the exponent math? Use the compound interest calculator to test different assumptions and compare results instantly.

Example: $10,000 at 5% for 5 years, compounded annually

  • Year 1: $10,500
  • Year 2: $11,025
  • Year 3: $11,576
  • Year 4: $12,155
  • Year 5: $12,763

Total interest earned: $2,763
(That’s $263 more than simple interest — with the same rate and time.)

Big idea: Compounding gets stronger with time. The gap between simple and compound interest becomes much larger over 10–20+ years, which is why compounding matters so much in long-term saving and investing.

Example B: With monthly contributions (real-life saving scenario)

Many people don’t invest a lump sum — they contribute monthly. Compounding becomes more meaningful as contributions add up.

Why Compounding Can Be Easy to Underestimate

Compounding can be easy to underestimate because the early years look slow. At first, the difference between simple and compound interest may appear small enough to ignore, which makes it easy to delay saving or investing.

The problem is that compounding is not meant to impress you immediately. Its real strength shows up later, when earlier gains begin producing gains of their own. That is why starting earlier often matters more than trying to find a perfect rate.

This is also why regular contributions may be helpful compared with delaying saving. If you want to see how regular contributions can change the outcome, compare different timelines in the compound interest calculator and then set a target with the savings goal calculator.

Does Compounding Frequency Matter?

Yes — but usually the difference is smaller over short time periods. Over long periods, frequent compounding adds up.

Example: $10,000 at 5% for 5 years (annual vs monthly compounding)

  • Annual compounding: $12,762.82
  • Monthly compounding: $12,833.59

These figures compare the same nominal annual rate. With an identical advertised APY, the interest earned over a full year should not be compared this way because APY already incorporates the compounding frequency. If you want to understand why APY can look higher than APR even when the product sounds similar, read our guide to APR vs APY.

Important perspective: Compounding frequency affects the ending balance, but it is only one of several factors. A slightly better frequency cannot compensate for starting late, contributing irregularly, or ignoring fees and inflation.

Real-World Mistake Example

A common mistake is comparing two accounts or investments based only on the headline rate. For example, someone might see two options both showing “5%” and assume they are effectively identical, even though one compounds monthly and the other annually.

Another mistake is focusing on nominal growth but ignoring inflation. A balance can rise in dollar terms while the real purchasing power grows much more slowly. That is why it helps to compare your projected balance with the inflation calculator, especially for longer time horizons.

On the debt side, the reverse problem happens when borrowers underestimate how interest adds up over time. If you are evaluating payoff strategies, it helps to look at structured repayment options and compare approaches such as snowball vs avalanche debt payoff.

Where You’ll See Each Type in Real Life

Where simple interest shows up

  • Some loans that accrue simple interest on the outstanding balance (terms vary)
  • Quick interest-cost estimates
  • Simple-interest teaching examples with a fixed principal and no interim repayments

Where compound interest shows up

  • High-yield savings accounts, often with daily or monthly compounding
  • CDs, depending on the product terms
  • Illustrative investment growth when returns are reinvested; actual market returns are variable and can be negative
Debt warning: Compounding helps your savings, but it can make debt more expensive too. If you are carrying high-interest balances, use a strategy to pay off debt faster and compare monthly costs with the loan payment calculator.

Common Mistakes People Make

  • Confusing APR and APY: APY includes compounding; APR typically does not. See APR vs APY.
  • Ignoring time: A small rate difference can become a large dollar difference over decades.
  • Comparing returns without inflation: Real returns matter, not just nominal balances. Use the inflation calculator.
  • Not checking compounding frequency: Daily vs monthly can change results slightly.
  • Waiting too long to start: Delaying contributions can hurt more than choosing a slightly lower rate.

Key Takeaways

  • Simple interest grows at a fixed rate with no interest-on-interest effect.
  • Compound interest grows faster because interest can earn more interest over time.
  • Compounding frequency matters, but time and consistency usually matter more.
  • For savings with retained interest, compounding is directly relevant; for investing, it is an illustrative model rather than a promised return.

FAQ

What is the main difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal only. Compound interest adds previously earned interest to the balance, so later periods can earn interest on earlier interest. Both examples assume a constant rate and no additional deposits.

Does monthly compounding always beat annual compounding?

At the same nominal annual rate, principal, and term, monthly compounding produces a higher balance than annual compounding when interest is positive and remains in the account. If two accounts advertise the same APY, the comparison is different because APY already accounts for compounding.

Is simple interest used for loans?

Yes. Some loans calculate simple interest on the outstanding principal. An amortizing loan also typically calculates periodic interest on its declining balance; that is not the same as using the flat I = P × r × t example for the original balance over the whole loan term.

How does inflation affect the results?

Inflation reduces purchasing power. A growing account balance does not guarantee that the money will buy proportionally more goods and services. These examples show nominal interest before taxes and fees.

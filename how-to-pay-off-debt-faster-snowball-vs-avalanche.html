Quick answer: the trade-off is interest versus motivation

Debt avalanche targets the highest interest rate (APR) first. With the same monthly budget and consistent payments, it generally minimizes interest. Debt snowball targets the smallest balance first. It can provide a quicker first win and may feel easier to keep up.

Neither method changes your contractual minimum-payment obligations: pay at least the required amount on every debt before directing extra money to the selected target. Late fees or penalty rates can outweigh the benefit of either strategy.

In the worked example below, avalanche saves $174.52 in interest and pays everything off one month earlier. Snowball clears the first account earlier.

How debt snowball and debt avalanche work

Snowball: smallest balance firstPay the minimum on each account. Put remaining money toward the smallest outstanding balance. After it is paid, redirect the freed payment toward the next smallest balance.
Avalanche: highest APR firstPay the minimum on each account. Direct everything extra to the debt with the highest APR. Once it is repaid, move to the next-highest APR.

For both methods, try to keep the total monthly debt-payment budget constant as accounts close. That is what allows the freed payments to accelerate repayment.

Here, choosing avalanche rather than snowball saves $174.52 in interest and reduces the overall payoff timeline by 1 month. Snowball, however, delivers its first paid-off account 9 months earlier. Both use exactly the same total payment budget until the last, smaller payment.

The size of the difference is specific to these balances, rates and minimums. Your outcome may be larger, smaller, or tied, especially when interest rates are similar.

Which payoff approach should you choose?

Choose avalanche when…You want to minimize interest, have a stable repayment routine, and can tolerate slower account closures.
Choose snowball when…You find it difficult to stay motivated with many open balances, and closing an account early helps you keep going.

A practical hybrid strategy

If you have a very small balance you can clear quickly, you could eliminate that balance for motivation, then switch to highest APR first. This generally costs more interest than a strict avalanche, but may make the plan easier to follow. Calculate the trade-off before choosing.

Five common mistakes to avoid

  • Missing a minimum payment: both methods require minimums on every open account.
  • Adding new balances: new credit-card purchases can undo progress.
  • Letting the budget shrink after a payoff: redirect the money to the next target if affordable.
  • Ignoring an emergency buffer: a modest reserve may help prevent new debt when an unexpected expense occurs.
  • Overlooking actual loan terms: check variable APRs, fees, promotional-rate expiry, and prepayment rules.
If you have no cash reserve, consider the Emergency Fund Calculator and How to Build an Emergency Fund. Balance emergency liquidity against the high cost of revolving debt.

Frequently asked questions

Is avalanche always cheaper?

Under identical payment budgets and standard positive interest rates, prioritizing the highest APR generally minimizes interest. Exceptions can arise when loans have fees, promotional terms, prepayment penalties, special rules, or changing interest rates.

Is snowball better for motivation?

For some people, yes. Paying off a small balance earlier creates a visible milestone. It is a behavioral benefit, not a guarantee that everyone will stick with the method.

What if two debts have the same APR?

If rates are equal and there are no special charges or restrictions, the interest advantage of paying one before the other may be negligible. Using the smaller balance as a tiebreaker can simplify the plan.

Can I switch methods later?

Yes. Re-rank remaining balances and continue making every minimum payment. Review the repayment plan when APRs, balances, or your monthly budget change.

Should I save or pay off debt first?

It depends on emergency savings, income stability, and interest rates. Keeping a modest emergency buffer while paying down expensive debt can be sensible; there is no universal amount suitable for everyone.

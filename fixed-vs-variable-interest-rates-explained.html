Fixed vs Variable Interest Rates: Costs, Risks & Real-World Examples

A practical guide to predictable payments, adjustable-rate mortgages, borrowing costs, and what to compare before choosing a loan.

FinanceCalcCenter educational guide · Examples in US dollars (USD)

Two loans can have the same amount and repayment term but very different risks. With a fixed interest rate, the rate stays unchanged for an agreed period. With a variable interest rate, it can move up or down under the terms of the contract. The lower starting rate is not necessarily the cheaper choice over the full life of the loan.

Quick answer: A fully fixed-rate mortgage provides more predictable principal-and-interest payments. A variable-rate mortgage may offer a lower initial rate or benefit from declining rates, but future payments may rise. Compare not just today's payment, but the reset rules, fees, loan duration, and a payment increase you could realistically afford.

What Is a Fixed Interest Rate?

A fixed interest rate does not change during the period for which it is locked in. On a fully fixed-rate mortgage, the rate lasts for the entire loan term. Other products may fix the rate for only a defined initial period; check what happens afterward.

For a standard fully amortizing fixed-rate mortgage, the scheduled principal-and-interest payment stays level as long as the loan terms do not change. Your total housing payment can still change if property taxes, insurance, or escrow charges change.

Advantages

  • Predictable principal-and-interest payments.
  • Protection from market-rate increases during the fixed period.
  • More straightforward long-term cash-flow planning.

Trade-offs

  • The initial rate may be higher than a comparable adjustable-rate offer.
  • If market rates fall, your contractual rate does not automatically decrease.
  • Refinancing may involve eligibility requirements, closing costs, and a new loan term.
Example: On a $200,000, 30-year mortgage fixed at 6%, the monthly principal-and-interest payment is about $1,199.10. This excludes property taxes, homeowners insurance, fees, and other housing costs.

What Is a Variable Interest Rate?

A variable (or adjustable) interest rate can change according to a loan's agreed rules. A US adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM), for example, commonly starts with a fixed-rate introductory period and then adjusts at specified intervals.

Some variable-rate savings accounts also change their rates, but savings products and mortgage loans operate differently. The comparisons and calculations below focus mainly on US mortgages.

Advantages

  • Some offers begin with a lower rate than comparable fully fixed-rate loans, but not always.
  • If the index declines, a future rate adjustment may reduce payments, subject to contract terms.
  • It can be useful when the introductory period and your plans align, provided you can manage reset risk.

Trade-offs

  • Monthly payments may increase when rates reset.
  • The total borrowing cost is less predictable.
  • Index, margin, caps, floors, and adjustment schedules can make comparisons complicated.

How Much Does the Interest Rate Change Your Monthly Payment?

Consider a $200,000 loan repaid over 30 years in 360 equal monthly principal-and-interest payments. The following figures assume each stated rate remains unchanged for the full 30 years. This is a fixed-rate sensitivity comparison, not a forecast of an ARM's future cost.

Annual rateMonthly paymentTotal interest
5.0%$1,073.64$186,512
6.0%$1,199.10$231,676
7.0%$1,330.60$279,018

Moving from 5% to 6% adds approximately $125.46 per month and about $45,165 in interest over the full term under these assumptions. The final amounts are rounded; calculations use unrounded payments.

Method: standard monthly amortization, payment = P × r / [1 − (1 + r)−n], where P is the starting balance, r is the annual nominal interest rate divided by 12, and n is the number of monthly payments. Taxes, insurance, upfront costs, loan fees and mortgage insurance are not included.

To try a different balance or repayment term, use the Mortgage Calculator or Loan Payment Calculator. Check each calculator's assumptions before comparing its output with an actual lender quote.

How Does an Adjustable-Rate Mortgage (ARM) Work?

ARM labels describe the initial fixed period and how frequently the rate adjusts afterward. For example, a traditional 5/1 ARM has an introductory rate for five years, followed by possible annual adjustments. A 7/1 ARM has an initial seven-year period, then possible annual adjustments. Other structures, including six-month adjustment intervals, also exist; always read the actual loan terms.

Index + margin

After the introductory period, the contractual rate is generally based on a published index plus the lender's fixed margin, subject to caps and floors. For instance, an illustrative index of 4.0% plus a margin of 2.5 percentage points would yield a fully indexed rate of 6.5% before applying contractual limits. This is an example, not a current market quote.

Interest rate caps and floors

ARM contracts may limit the first adjustment, subsequent adjustments, and the lifetime increase. A floor may limit how far the rate can fall. The maximum payment is therefore determined by the specific loan contract, not by an assumed market scenario.

Important: Do not assume you can sell your home or refinance before the adjustable period. Home values, credit, income, and lending conditions could change. Ask your lender for the maximum permitted payment and review the Loan Estimate and ARM disclosures.

Real-Life Scenario: What If a Variable Rate Changes?

Imagine a hypothetical 30-year, $200,000 loan that starts at 5% for five years. Its initial monthly principal-and-interest payment is $1,073.64. After 60 scheduled payments, the balance is approximately $183,657.46.

The choice involves a trade-off: a potentially lower initial payment versus uncertainty after the introductory period. Compare complete lender quotes, not only these sample payments.

When Might Each Type Make Sense?

A fixed rate may fit better when…

  • You need stable principal-and-interest payments to manage your budget.
  • You intend to hold the mortgage for many years.
  • A large future payment increase would create financial strain.
  • You prefer certainty over a potentially lower starting rate.

A variable rate may be worth evaluating when…

  • The initial savings are meaningful after comparing fees and loan terms.
  • You understand the reset dates, index, margin, caps, and maximum possible payment.
  • You have enough financial flexibility to absorb an increase.
  • Your intended holding period is short, but you could still afford the loan if plans change.

Five questions to ask a lender

  1. What is the initial rate, APR, and total closing cost for each offer?
  2. Exactly when can the rate adjust, and which index and margin apply?
  3. What are the initial, periodic, and lifetime caps or floors?
  4. How high could the principal-and-interest payment rise under the contract?
  5. Are there prepayment penalties, refinancing fees, or other features that affect the total cost?

The interest rate and annual percentage rate (APR) are not the same: APR generally incorporates certain borrowing charges. APR can help compare costs, but it does not tell you the maximum rate an adjustable loan could reach.

Common Questions and Misconceptions

Are variable rates always cheaper?

No. A lower introductory payment can be offset by later increases, fees, or a longer repayment period. Actual savings depend on how the rate evolves and when you repay the loan.

Is a fixed-rate mortgage completely risk-free?

No. Rate stability does not eliminate affordability, income, property-value, or homeownership risks. Property taxes and insurance may still increase.

Will a variable-rate payment always fall when market rates fall?

Not necessarily. Adjustments happen under contractual schedules and may be constrained by a floor, caps, or other loan features.

Should I choose an ARM if I plan to refinance?

Do not base the decision solely on refinancing. Approval and attractive terms are not guaranteed in the future. Consider whether you can afford the loan if refinancing is unavailable.

Does fixed versus variable matter for savings accounts?

Yes, but the perspective reverses: on a savings product, interest is money you may earn. A fixed-rate deposit may lock in a return for a set term, while a variable-rate savings account may change its yield. Check access restrictions, fees, and any early-withdrawal penalties.

Bottom Line

Fixed rates primarily provide payment predictability; variable rates offer potential benefits if rates fall or the introductory terms are favorable, in exchange for reset risk. The better option depends on the full contract, your budget, how long you might keep the loan, and whether you can manage a higher payment.

Before deciding, compare lender-provided Loan Estimates, stress-test the payment, and examine both the interest rate and APR. Online examples are useful starting points, but they cannot replace the terms of a specific offer.

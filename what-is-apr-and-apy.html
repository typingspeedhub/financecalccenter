What Are APR and APY?

APR and APY are two common ways of expressing annual interest. This guide explains what each term means, where you are likely to see it, and why compounding matters when interest is earned or charged.

Updated: September 5, 2026 Reading time: 7–9 minutes Educational use only

Written by Tibor Győry

Interest rates are often presented with abbreviations that look similar but describe different ideas. Two of the most common are APR and APY. Understanding the words behind those abbreviations makes loan and savings disclosures much easier to read.

In simple terms: APR stands for Annual Percentage Rate and is commonly associated with borrowing. APY stands for Annual Percentage Yield and is commonly used for savings and deposit products because it reflects compounding.

What Does APR Mean?

APR means Annual Percentage Rate. It expresses a borrowing rate on an annual basis. You will often see APR when a lender or card issuer describes the cost of credit.

APR is not always identical to the basic interest rate shown elsewhere in a loan offer. Depending on the product and applicable disclosure rules, APR can include certain finance charges or required fees. That is one reason APR is often useful when reading borrowing offers.

Example: a credit card with a 24% APR does not mean that 24% is added to the balance every month. It is an annualized rate. The actual interest charged depends on the balance, the card's daily or periodic rate, billing-cycle rules, and whether a grace period applies.

Where Do You Usually See APR?

APR is commonly used on borrowing products such as:

  • credit cards
  • personal loans
  • auto loans
  • mortgages
  • student loans
  • installment financing

The exact way APR is calculated or disclosed can vary by product and jurisdiction, so the accompanying terms still matter. Fees, loan length, variable-rate conditions, and repayment rules can all affect your total cost.

What Does APY Mean?

APY means Annual Percentage Yield. It expresses the effective annual return on money that earns interest, including the effect of compounding.

Compounding means previously earned interest can itself begin earning interest. Because APY includes that effect, it gives savers a convenient way to understand the annual yield of an account under the stated assumptions.

Example: if a savings account advertises a 5.00% APY, the 5.00% figure already reflects the stated compounding effect over one year, assuming the rate and account conditions remain unchanged.

Where Do You Usually See APY?

APY commonly appears on deposit and cash products such as:

  • traditional savings accounts
  • high-yield savings accounts
  • certificates of deposit (CDs)
  • money market accounts
  • some cash-management or interest-bearing accounts

APY is useful because it turns the stated rate and compounding schedule into one annual percentage. However, it does not tell you everything about an account. Minimum balances, fees, withdrawal rules, promotional periods, and variable rates can still affect the result you actually receive.

How Does Compounding Affect APY?

Compounding frequency describes how often earned interest is added to the balance. Common schedules include annual, quarterly, monthly, and daily compounding.

APY = (1 + r / n)^n - 1

In this simplified formula, r is the stated annual rate as a decimal and n is the number of compounding periods per year.

For example, a nominal annual rate of 5% compounded monthly produces an effective annual yield slightly above 5%. More frequent compounding can increase APY, although the difference may be modest over a single year.

Interest Rate vs APR: Why the Terms Can Differ

A common source of confusion is the difference between an advertised interest rate and an APR. On a loan, the interest rate usually describes the rate charged on the principal, while APR may provide a broader annualized measure that includes certain costs tied to borrowing.

This is especially relevant with mortgages and some installment loans. Two products can have similar interest rates but different APRs if their fees or finance charges differ.

Important: APR rules depend on the financial product and local regulations. Always read the lender's official disclosure rather than assuming every APR includes the same types of fees.

How to Read APR and APY Information

When you see APR or APY in an offer, first identify what kind of product you are looking at. A borrowing product and a savings product use annual percentage figures for different purposes.

For a loan or credit card, check:

  • whether the rate is fixed or variable
  • origination, closing, or other required fees
  • the repayment term
  • the monthly payment
  • the total amount repaid
  • special rates for cash advances or balance transfers

For a savings or deposit account, check:

  • whether the APY is promotional or ongoing
  • minimum balance requirements
  • monthly maintenance fees
  • withdrawal or access restrictions
  • whether the rate can change
  • deposit-insurance or account-protection details that apply in your country

APR and APY Are Not Direct Substitutes

APR and APY are both annual percentage measures, but they are designed for different contexts. This article focuses on what the two terms mean. Frequently Asked Questions

What does APR stand for?

APR stands for Annual Percentage Rate. It is an annualized measure commonly used to describe the cost of borrowing.

What does APY stand for?

APY stands for Annual Percentage Yield. It expresses the effective annual yield on an interest-bearing balance after compounding.

Does APY include compounding?

Yes. APY incorporates the effect of compounding over a one-year period under the stated account assumptions.

Does APR include fees?

It can include certain fees or finance charges, depending on the product and applicable disclosure rules. Always check the official terms for the specific loan or credit product.

Can APR and APY change over time?

Yes. Variable-rate loans, credit cards, and savings accounts can change over time according to their terms.

Final Thoughts

APR and APY become much easier to understand once you separate their basic purpose. APR is a common borrowing-rate measure, while APY is an annual yield measure that includes compounding.

These percentages are useful starting points, but they should always be read together with the product's fees, terms, rate conditions, and other disclosures.

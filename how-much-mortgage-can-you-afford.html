How Much Mortgage Can You Afford?

Buying a home is one of the largest financial decisions most people make. Before looking at listings, it helps to estimate a monthly housing payment that fits comfortably within your income and existing obligations.

The amount a lender may approve and the amount you can comfortably afford are not always the same. Your income, monthly debts, down payment, interest rate, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and other ownership costs all affect the final result.

This guide explains common mortgage affordability rules, shows realistic examples, and helps you build a safer home-buying budget.

What Determines Mortgage Affordability?

Mortgage affordability is based on more than the purchase price of a home. Lenders and borrowers usually consider several connected factors:

  • Gross monthly income
  • Existing monthly debt payments
  • Credit history and credit score
  • Down payment amount
  • Mortgage interest rate
  • Loan term
  • Property taxes
  • Homeowners insurance
  • Private mortgage insurance, if required
  • HOA fees and other recurring housing costs

Even a small change in the interest rate, down payment, or monthly debt payment can noticeably change the amount of mortgage you may be able to support.

The 28/36 Rule

A commonly used budgeting guideline is the 28/36 rule. It is not a universal lending requirement, but it can provide a useful starting point.

  • Housing costs should generally stay near or below 28% of gross monthly income.
  • Total monthly debt payments should generally stay near or below 36% of gross monthly income.

Housing costs may include principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and sometimes HOA fees. Total debt may also include car loans, student loans, credit card minimum payments, personal loans, and other required obligations.

Example
  • Gross monthly income: $6,000
  • 28% housing limit: $1,680
  • 36% total debt limit: $2,160
  • Existing monthly debt payments: $400

The total-debt calculation leaves:

$2,160 − $400 = $1,760

However, the separate housing guideline is lower at $1,680. Under the 28/36 guideline, the estimated maximum housing payment would therefore be approximately:

$1,680 per month

A more conservative household may choose a lower percentage to leave additional room for savings, repairs, travel, childcare, or other goals.

Understanding Debt-to-Income Ratio

The debt-to-income ratio, often shortened to DTI, compares required monthly debt payments with gross monthly income.

Monthly Required Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income × 100
Example
  • Gross monthly income: $5,000
  • Required monthly debt payments: $1,500
$1,500 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 30%

In this example, the borrower has a 30% debt-to-income ratio.

Acceptable DTI limits vary by lender, mortgage program, credit profile, cash reserves, down payment, and other underwriting factors. A guideline should therefore be treated as an estimate rather than a guaranteed approval standard.

How Interest Rates Affect Affordability

Interest rates have a major effect on the monthly payment. A higher rate increases the cost of borrowing and reduces the loan amount that can fit within the same monthly budget.

Illustrative example: $350,000 mortgage over 30 years
Interest rate Approximate principal and interest
5% About $1,879 per month
7% About $2,329 per month

The difference is roughly $450 per month, before property taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, or HOA fees.

The Impact of Your Down Payment

A larger down payment reduces the amount you need to borrow. This can lower the monthly payment, reduce total interest costs, and sometimes eliminate the need for private mortgage insurance.

Example: $400,000 home
  • 5% down payment: $20,000 down and a $380,000 loan
  • 20% down payment: $80,000 down and a $320,000 loan

The second buyer borrows $60,000 less. That can reduce the monthly principal-and-interest payment by several hundred dollars, depending on the interest rate and loan term.

A larger down payment is not automatically the best choice for every household. Buyers should also consider closing costs, emergency savings, moving expenses, and the need for repairs or furniture after purchase.

A Realistic Affordability Example

Example buyer
  • Annual gross income: $90,000
  • Gross monthly income: $7,500
  • Existing monthly debt payments: $0

Using the 28% guideline:

$7,500 × 28% = $2,100

This $2,100 represents an estimated total monthly housing budget, not just principal and interest.

Suppose approximately $400 to $600 per month is reserved for property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, or other required housing costs. That would leave roughly $1,500 to $1,700 for principal and interest.

At a 6.5% interest rate on a 30-year fixed mortgage, that payment range could support a loan of approximately:

$235,000 to $270,000

This is an illustration only. Actual taxes, insurance premiums, loan fees, down payment requirements, and lender standards vary.

Do Not Confuse Loan Approval With Affordability

A lender may approve a payment that uses a large portion of your monthly income. That does not necessarily mean the payment will feel comfortable after groceries, utilities, transportation, healthcare, childcare, savings, and unexpected expenses.

Important: The maximum amount you qualify to borrow is not automatically the maximum amount you should borrow.

A safer approach is to choose a payment that still allows you to save, maintain an emergency fund, and handle home repairs without relying on credit cards.

Hidden Homeownership Costs

The mortgage payment is only one part of the cost of owning a home. Buyers should also plan for:

  • Property taxes
  • Homeowners insurance
  • Private mortgage insurance
  • HOA fees
  • Utilities
  • Routine maintenance
  • Major repairs
  • Closing costs
  • Moving costs
  • Furniture and appliances

These costs can add hundreds of dollars per month and may vary significantly by location and property type.

How Much Should You Reserve for Maintenance?

There is no single maintenance rule that works for every property. Older homes, large homes, and properties with aging roofs, heating systems, plumbing, or appliances may require a larger reserve.

Rather than relying on one percentage, review the age and condition of the home and build a separate repair fund. A home inspection can help identify upcoming expenses, but it cannot predict every future problem.

Common Mortgage Affordability Mistakes

  • Looking only at principal and interest
  • Ignoring property taxes and insurance
  • Using the maximum lender approval as a target
  • Forgetting closing and moving costs
  • Draining all savings for the down payment
  • Underestimating maintenance and repairs
  • Assuming interest rates will fall later
  • Failing to budget for income changes

A mortgage may appear affordable on paper but still create financial stress if there is no room for emergencies or other important goals.

How Mortgage Payments Are Calculated

A typical monthly housing payment may include:

  • Principal: the portion that reduces the loan balance
  • Interest: the cost charged by the lender
  • Property taxes: local taxes associated with the home
  • Homeowners insurance: coverage for the property
  • Mortgage insurance: sometimes required with a smaller down payment
  • HOA fees: applicable to some communities and buildings

How to Choose a Comfortable Mortgage Budget

  1. Start with your gross monthly income.
  2. Calculate a rough housing limit using a conservative percentage.
  3. Subtract existing required monthly debts.
  4. Estimate taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, and HOA fees.
  5. Keep emergency savings after the down payment and closing costs.
  6. Test the budget against your real monthly spending.
  7. Leave room for repairs, savings, and changing expenses.

Buying below your maximum affordability level can provide more financial flexibility and reduce the risk of becoming house poor.

Questions to Ask Before Buying

  • Would this payment still be manageable if one expense increased?
  • Will I still be able to save every month?
  • Do I have money left after the down payment and closing costs?
  • Can I afford an unexpected repair?
  • Am I relying on overtime or bonuses to make the payment work?
  • Would a less expensive home better support my other goals?

Final Thoughts

Estimating how much mortgage you can afford requires more than comparing income with a home price. Your existing debts, down payment, interest rate, taxes, insurance, maintenance costs, and personal financial goals all matter.

The 28/36 rule and debt-to-income ratio can provide useful starting points, but they should not replace a complete household budget. A home that leaves room for savings and unexpected expenses is often a safer choice than the maximum loan a lender is willing to approve.
